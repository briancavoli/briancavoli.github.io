The Next Generation of Home Care

From AI Adoption to Continuous Improvement

Stronger businesses. Better jobs. Better care.

By Brian Cavoli

Five years ago, I entered the home care industry because I saw enormous potential for innovation.

Home care is an extraordinary business. It delivers something deeply personal and meaningful, supported by dedicated people doing challenging work. I also saw an industry with significant opportunities to modernize how it operates, markets itself, and grows.

Many processes remain remarkably manual. Information is fragmented across systems, conversations, and people. And despite meaningful differences between agencies, much of the industry’s marketing looks and sounds surprisingly similar.

The same promises of compassionate care. The same stock photography. The same claims of exceptional service and being the best.

These are important promises, but when everyone says essentially the same thing, it’s a sea of sameness. How does a family understand what makes one agency different from another?

I saw an opportunity for greater sophistication, more creativity, stronger differentiation, and more effective business practices.

Today, that opportunity is becoming more tangible than ever.

AI and emerging technologies are creating possibilities that were difficult to imagine just a few years ago. But the real opportunity isn’t simply the technology.

It’s what happens when experienced people combine new capabilities with strategy, creativity, better workflows, and a commitment to continuous improvement.

We’re entering a new generation of home care.

Beyond Automation: Reinventing How Agencies Work

Home care is inherently complex. Every client has different needs, every caregiver brings different strengths, and circumstances can change quickly.

It’s also a business that depends heavily on human coordination. Scheduling, recruiting, client intake, caregiver communication, documentation, and reporting all involve activities that can be time-consuming and difficult to manage.

AI offers tremendous opportunities to simplify and automate this work.

But automation alone isn’t transformation.

Automating an inefficient process may simply make it run faster. A better reporting tool doesn’t necessarily create better decisions. And introducing an AI assistant doesn’t mean employees will know how to incorporate it into their work.

The opportunity starts with understanding how work gets done and asking what could be done differently.

Where are employees spending time without creating much value? What decisions depend too heavily on intuition? What could we learn from the information we’re already collecting?

Then we can redesign workflows, introduce technology where it adds value, and help people work in new ways.

Done well, this creates more than efficiency. It gives employees more capacity to focus on work that requires human judgment, problem-solving, relationships, and care.

Beyond Sameness: Reinventing How Agencies Go to Market

The opportunity for innovation is just as significant in marketing and customer experience.

Home care is an intensely personal service, yet much of its marketing feels impersonal.

Agencies frequently make nearly identical promises about quality, compassion, trust, and outstanding caregivers. Those qualities matter enormously, but they’re often presented as claims rather than demonstrated through meaningful evidence.

Families facing difficult care decisions need more than reassuring adjectives.

They want to know whether an agency can meet their needs, how it selects caregivers, what makes its approach different, and why they should have confidence in the people entering their home.

They want clarity, credibility, and certainty.

These are opportunities for stronger positioning, better storytelling, more relevant communications, and a more sophisticated approach to growth.

AI can make customer insights more accessible, accelerate content development, enable personalization, and help marketers test and refine ideas.

But there’s a catch.

AI can either accelerate the sameness or help break organizations out of it. The difference is the thinking behind its application.

Give everyone the same tools and generic prompts, and we may simply produce more of the same content, faster.

Put those capabilities in the hands of experienced marketers who understand customers, strategy, differentiation, and creativity, and the possibilities are very different.

More distinctive brands. Stronger stories. Greater relevance. More personality and character.

And the ability to continually learn what resonates and drives results.

The goal shouldn’t be to create more marketing. It should be to create better marketing.

The Continuous Improvement Flywheel

This brings me to what I believe is the most exciting opportunity of all.

AI doesn’t just give home care organizations new tools. It gives them new ways to understand their businesses, learn from experience, and systematically improve performance.

I’ve always appreciated the flywheel concept: the idea that sustainable progress comes from reinforcing activities that build momentum over time.

I believe this is a powerful model for the next generation of home care.

  1. Understand the business.

    Every day, agencies generate valuable information through client inquiries, caregiver interactions, care notes, referral relationships, scheduling, recruiting, and financial systems.

    Much of this information has historically been difficult to connect or analyze. We know what happened, but often lack a complete understanding of why.

  2. Uncover new insights.

    AI and better analytics make it possible to identify patterns and ask questions that previously would have been difficult or impractical to answer.

    Why do certain referral partnerships outperform others? What makes some sales conversations more successful? Where are caregivers encountering friction? Which operational practices create better experiences?

  3. Turn insights into action.

    This is where transformation happens.

    Insights inform changes in workflows, training, messaging, responsibilities, collaboration, and management practices.

    We don’t just give people more information. We help them do something better with it.

  4. Measure, learn, and refine.

    Did the change improve performance? Did it make employees more effective? Did it create better experiences for clients or caregivers?

    We measure what matters, learn from the results, and adjust.

Then the cycle begins again.

Each improvement generates new information. Each insight creates opportunities to improve.

Over time, the organization develops something more valuable than a collection of technology tools.

It develops the ability to continuously learn, adapt, and get better.

What This Looks Like in Practice

I’ve seen the potential of this approach firsthand.

At a multi-location home care organization, I helped build marketing, sales, and performance measurement capabilities from the ground up.

As those capabilities evolved, we began uncovering insights that challenged long-held assumptions about how the business generated growth.

In referral partner development, for example, our analysis revealed that developing a relationship into a meaningful revenue source could take nine to ten months.

That changed how we thought about partner value.

Rather than focusing primarily on recent referral activity, we began examining the characteristics of successful relationships, the individuals generating opportunities, the types of care they referred, and the value of developing multiple relationships within an organization.

Those insights helped shape a more strategic approach to account development.

In client sales, AI-assisted analysis of phone conversations and CRM notes revealed patterns associated with successful and unsuccessful inquiries.

One finding stood out.

What families wanted most was certainty.

Could we provide the care they needed? Were we available? Could we deliver the right caregiver? Why was our premium service worth the price?

When our teams clearly communicated benefits and addressed those concerns confidently, conversion tended to be stronger. Hesitation and vague answers could undermine confidence.

We also discovered that many families knew so little about home care they didn’t even know which questions to ask.

These insights informed new training, coaching, sales tools, messaging, and educational website content.

The value wasn’t in the AI analysis alone.

It was in what we learned and how we used that learning to change our approach.

That’s the flywheel in action.

Stronger Businesses. Better Jobs. Better Care.

The implications extend far beyond marketing and sales.

Imagine scheduling teams spending less time on routine coordination and more time supporting caregivers.

Recruiters better understanding what makes a successful hire and improving the candidate experience.

Caregivers receiving clearer information, better training, and more responsive support.

Care teams recognizing changes in client needs sooner and having more informed conversations with families.

These outcomes won’t happen automatically. They require thoughtful workflow design, responsible technology use, strong leadership, and employees who help shape the changes.

Because home care is, and always will be, a people business.

Technology should strengthen the relationships that make home care valuable, not replace them.

Better operations can create more capacity to support employees. Better-supported employees can deliver better experiences. Better experiences can build stronger relationships, reputations, and businesses.

Each reinforces the other.

Building What’s Next

I don’t believe the next generation of home care will be defined by which companies adopt the most AI tools.

The competitive advantage will come from how effectively organizations put those capabilities to work.

That requires leaders who connect growth strategy with operations, information with action, and innovation with the people who make the business work.

It requires curiosity, a willingness to challenge assumptions, and a commitment to learning from what the business is telling us.

And it requires patience. Implementing technology can be relatively fast. Building new habits, capabilities, and ways of thinking takes longer.

Throughout my career, I’ve been drawn to opportunities created by changes in marketing, technology, and customer behavior.

What excites me about this moment in home care is the chance to bring those experiences together in an industry where better business performance can make a meaningful difference in people’s lives.

We have an opportunity to move beyond manual processes, generic marketing, disconnected information, and reactive decision-making.

To build more sophisticated organizations with distinctive brands, empowered employees, smarter operations, and a culture of continuous improvement.

AI and emerging technologies are helping make that future possible.

But people, leadership, creativity, and the willingness to change will determine how much of that potential we realize.

Stronger businesses.Better jobs.Better care.

That’s the next generation of home care I want to help build.
